How the final graph gets built

Three quantities get multiplied together and summed over every energy above Ec or below Ev. Nothing here is fitted to the picture above — moving the sliders re-runs this exact integral.

1

Count the available states

Near a band edge, the density of states grows as the square root of distance from the edge — more energy levels open up the further you move into the band.

2

Ask how likely each one is filled

The Fermi–Dirac function gives the occupation probability of a state at energy E. A hole is simply a state the electron probability leaves empty.

3

Multiply, then add it all up

Multiplying states available by probability filled, then integrating across the band, gives the carrier concentration — this is the "final graph" on the right.

Why metals barely notice temperature

In a metal, the Fermi level sits deep inside a band that is already full of states below it, at every temperature you'd realistically apply. Warming the material only blurs the occupation edge right around EF — the electron count barely moves, which is why the electron bar above stays pinned near full as you drag the slider.

Resistance in a real metal actually climbs with temperature, but for a different reason — more lattice vibrations mean more scattering. That effect isn't part of this density-of-states picture.

Why semiconductors wake up as they heat

Push the Fermi level into the gap and there are no states there for it to fill. The only way electrons reach the conduction band is by being thermally kicked across Eg — and the Fermi function's tail beyond Ec is exponentially sensitive to kBT.

That's why n and p above can climb by orders of magnitude between 300 K and 1000 K, and why conductivity in a semiconductor rises with heat instead of falling. Switch to Insulator and try the same thing — the gap is just too wide for that tail to reach.
